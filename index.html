  1. IAFFE Annual Conference Cali, Colombia, July 2026

    Citi biking and time savings: the cost of Brooklyn's missing bike lanes

    New York City's Manhattan-centric subway system creates a transit gap for north-south Brooklyn commuters. With no direct train route, riders are forced to take a roundabout, 50-minute trip into Manhattan just to transfer back out to Brooklyn.

    Anecdotally, biking cuts this commute in half. But the routes filling this transit gap lack protected bike lanes, forcing riders to bike in car traffic and making the journey dangerous. Most studies agree this physical risk disproportionately deters women, who currently make up just 25% of Citi Bike riders.

    Approach and data

    This research asks: how does women's Citi Bike participation change when massive time savings are on the line?

    • Uses 2019 Citi Bike data: the last year gender was included in the public datasets
    • Isolate north-south trips with maximum time savings and build meaningful control groups to examine how women's ridership shifts when time savings are present

    Research questions

    Q1: Do women riders undertake north-south commutes in Central Brooklyn at a higher rate than on comparable, matched control routes?

    Q2: How much time does cycling save compared to the subway on north-south routes in Central Brooklyn versus on matched control routes, and does this time savings differ between men and women riders?

  2. NYC Open Data Week Workshop, March 2026

    Feeding New York: the hidden burden on the South Bronx

    New York City’s food supply chain relies on a distribution system dependent on diesel trucks, creating compounding environmental and economic burdens in the South Bronx. This project examines Hunts Point, home to the city’s largest wholesale food distribution hub, through the intersecting lenses of air quality, poverty, and climate risk. It critically evaluates environmental data, tests the statistical significance of neighborhood-level air quality differences, and examines the limitations of relying on existing monitoring systems as “ground truth.”

    Looking forward, the project explores policy alternatives to the current model. By mapping Metro-North and LIRR rail lines alongside regional farmland and underserved communities, it examines the untapped potential of existing transit infrastructure to reduce diesel emissions and improve food access. With portions of Hunts Point projected to flood under future climate scenarios, change is not just desirable; it is inevitable.

  3. NYC Open Data Week Workshop, March 2025

    Mapping iconic NYC sandwiches

    Sandwiches are my passion. When the New York Times unveiled its list of 57 sandwiches that define New York City, I wanted to try them all. The problem: the NYT list only provides sandwich names and restaurant addresses. Determining if I’m near an iconic sandwich requires scrolling, reading, and flipping between the list and Google Maps. The solution: I need a sandwich map!

    Using a large language model (LLM) as a pair programming partner, we built the map using Python, web scraping, Google Maps, and GitHub Pages.

  4. Levy Economics Institute of Bard College Master's thesis, May 2024

    In transit: how public transportation affects the gender leisure gap

    Women in the US have shorter commutes than men but still get less free time each day. I combined the American Time Use Survey with the National Household Travel Survey to study how commuting, household errands, and transit use relate to leisure for full-time workers who live with a partner.

    Each extra minute of commuting cost about 45 seconds of leisure, and each extra minute of household travel cost about 39 seconds. Women had 46 fewer minutes of leisure a day than men, even after accounting for travel and children. Transit commutes took twice as long as driving, but transit riders didn't have less leisure.
